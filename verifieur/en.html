The goal, in one sentence

Make it possible to prove, later and to anyone, that a given document already existed at a given time and has not been modified since — without ever publishing the document itself.

It is the digital equivalent of a dated wax seal, except that the "register" dating the seal is public and tamper-proof (the Bitcoin blockchain), and anyone can redo the verification without taking the platform's word for it.

The four basic ideas

1. The fingerprint

A document is never published. Instead we compute its fingerprint: a kind of 64-character digital fingerprint, produced by a standard mathematical computation (called SHA-256). Two properties matter: the same document always gives the same fingerprint, and it is impossible to reconstruct the document from the fingerprint. This fingerprint — and nothing else — is what ever leaves the data owner's premises.

Beforehand, the data is written in a unique, normalized form (called "canonical"): same data ⇒ same text, byte for byte ⇒ same fingerprint, whatever software is used.

2. The salt

Problem: if a document is easy to guess (say, "grade: 12/20"), someone could try every possibility and compare fingerprints. The countermeasure: before computing, a salt is mixed into the document — a large secret random number, different for every document, kept by the data owner. Without the salt, nothing can be confirmed at all.

3. The tree (a "Merkle" tree)

Publishing one fingerprint per document would be costly. So fingerprints are combined two by two, then two by two again, until a single summary fingerprint remains: the root. Like a sports tournament: many participants, a single final. Only the root is published — and each document keeps the list of its "matches" (its path) to prove it leads to that root.

4. Anchoring

The root is deposited into the Bitcoin blockchain, via a free, standard service called OpenTimestamps. The blockchain is a public register where every page (a block) is numbered and impossible to rewrite. Once the root is in block number B, we can state: "everything that led to this root existed no later than when block B was written".

The witness: the final receipt

The witness (in French: témoin) is the receipt handed to the document's owner: a small JSON file containing everything needed to redo the verification — the data (in its canonical form), the salt, the path in the tree, the root, and the proof of the Bitcoin deposit (the attestation). Whoever holds a witness can prove their document's anteriority to anyone, even if the issuing platform has disappeared.

A witness JSON file, concretely

"JSON" is simply a text file format, readable by humans and machines alike: "name: value" pairs between braces. Here is a real witness — the one used by the example in the Verify tab — then each field in plain language. Note: the field names are French words (sel, racine…) because they are frozen technical identifiers defined by the public specification — like the English keywords found in most file formats, they are never translated: 

{
  "format": 1,
  "hachage": "sha-256",
  "canonique": "{\"n\":3}",
  "sel": "3333333333333333333333333333333333333333333333333333333333333333",
  "chemin": [
    { "cote": "gauche", "empreinte": "619d2265…4d659f35" }
  ],
  "racine": "c6c5f461…106ebe8e",
  "attestations": []
}
  • format — the version of the rulebook, so that a verifier in 2036 still knows how to read this receipt.
  • hachage — "hash": the fingerprint function used (SHA-256).
  • canonique — "canonical": the sealed data itself, in its unique normalized form.
  • sel — "salt": the secret number specific to this record, written in hexadecimal (64 characters for 32 bytes).
  • chemin — "path": the list of tournament "matches", from this document's fingerprint up to the summary, each saying whether the partner was on the left (gauche) or the right (droite).
  • racine — "root": the single summary fingerprint, the one deposited into Bitcoin.
  • attestations — the proofs of the deposit. Empty as here: the witness is awaiting anchoring and does not prove any anteriority yet.

What the proof guarantees — exactly

A valid witness proves: "these bytes existed no later than when block B was mined". Nothing more. We never say "on January 15 at 8:30am": the displayed time of a Bitcoin block can drift by about two hours — we only promise what we can keep. And a witness proves neither the accuracy nor the sincerity of the content: it proves the anteriority and integrity of precise bytes. That is why we speak of "records certified by public anchoring", never of "official" documents: legal value depends on applicable law; the technical proof, however, is strong.

What about the right to erasure? (GDPR)

The blockchain carries no personal data, ever — only salted fingerprints, mute by construction. The day data must be erased, the data and its salt are destroyed: the published fingerprint becomes permanently inert — it no longer allows anyone to recover or even confirm anything. This mechanism is called crypto-shredding: what is erasable lives off-chain, and what is on-chain never carried anything erasable.

A witness's life, in six stages

From document to verifiable receipt: what happens, in order, at each sealing. Stages 1 to 4 are pure offline computation; only stage 5 touches the Internet.

  1. Canonical form The data to seal (a grade, a diploma, any record) is written in its unique, normalized form: same data, same text, byte for byte.
  2. Salt A large secret random number is drawn for this specific record and entrusted to the data owner. It stays off-chain, stored and destroyed together with the data.
  3. Fingerprint The salt and the canonical data are mixed then passed through SHA-256: this is the record's digital fingerprint (its "leaf").
  4. Tree and root All the batch's leaves are combined two by two, tournament-style, down to the single summary fingerprint: the root. Each record keeps its path to it.
  5. Anchoring into Bitcoin The root — it alone — is deposited via OpenTimestamps. The proof is first incomplete (queued); a few hours later, after confirmation in a numbered block, it is completed. This is the only moment the system touches the network.
  6. Handing over the witness Each owner receives their receipt: canonical data + salt + path + root + attestation. From then on they can have everything verified, right here or with independent tools — forever.

Verify your witness, step by step

This guide is for everyone — student, employer, family, administration. No technical knowledge is needed: if you can open a file, you can verify a witness. It is illustrated with Sparkso Universités, whose first instance is IUGM in Mahajanga (the interfaces shown are in French).

  1. Find your witness file It is a small file that was handed to you (by your university, for example), whose name ends in .temoin.json. Keep it safe, like a personal document: it contains your data and a secret code.
  2. Open the verification page That's the page you are on. Each institution hosts it at its own address, but all copies are identical — and you can use any of them: the page only computes, on your machine, and sends nothing.
  3. Drop the file Go to the "Verify a witness" tab and drag your file into the dashed frame (or click the frame to pick it from your computer)tap the dashed frame to pick the file on your phone. Verification starts by itself, in under a second.
  4. Read the result A colored panel appears: green, amber or red. The exact meaning of each color is detailed right below this guide. Under the panel, every computation step is displayed and explained in plain language.
  5. If it's green: see for yourself The green panel shows a Bitcoin block number and a "View the block" link. Click it: you land on a public website independent from us (an "explorer"). If the block's page exists and carries the same number, you have your confirmation: your data existed no later than that block, and has not changed since.

The three possible results

● Green — "Consistent with its witness"

All is well: the computations confirm the witness, and the root is anchored in a specific Bitcoin block. There is nothing for you to do — your proof is solid and will remain so, even if the platform that issued it disappears one day.

● Amber — "Consistent, but no proof of anteriority yet"

The computations are correct, but nothing ties this witness to the blockchain yet: either anchoring has not happened yet, or it is awaiting Bitcoin confirmation (a few hours). This is a normal, temporary state right after a sealing. If it lasts several days, report it to the witness's issuer.

● Red — "Not consistent"

The computations do not confirm this witness: the file was modified, damaged (for instance during a copy), or is malformed. It does not mean your data is wrong — it means this particular file proves nothing. Ask the issuer to re-deliver your witness.

Frequently asked questions

How did I receive this file? Where do I find it?

Your institution hands it to you, usually through a "Download my witness" button in your personal account (one file per sealed item: transcript, diploma…). Once downloaded, it sits on your computer, often in the "Downloads" folder — look for a name ending in .temoin.json. There is nothing to "install": it is a plain file, yours, forever.

The file contains French words — does it work outside France?

Yes, everywhere. The words inside the file (temoin, racine, gauche…) are not "language": they are frozen technical identifiers, defined once and for all by the format's public specification — just like the English keywords found in most computer formats are never translated. Software from any country reads them as-is, and this page also exists in French: both verify exactly the same files.

The file's name doesn't matter technically either: renamed witness.json or diploma-2026.json, it verifies exactly the same — only the content counts. The .temoin.json ending is just a convention, to recognize it at a glance.

Does my witness file contain sensitive information?

Yes: it contains your data (readable) and the secret code protecting it. Treat it like a paper transcript or diploma: keep it at home, do not publish it. Handing it to someone means showing them your data — which is exactly what it is for, when you decide to.

I lost my witness file. Is that serious?

No: the institution that issued it keeps what is needed to re-deliver it identically. The proof inside Bitcoin, for its part, never moves.

Do I need the Internet to verify?

The computations (steps 1 to 4) work even offline. The Internet is needed for one thing only: clicking the link to the public explorer to observe the Bitcoin block.

Why should I trust this page?

You don't have to: it sends nothing anywhere (any IT person's tools can confirm that), and everything it computes can be recomputed by an independent program, written by anyone, from the system's public rules. Technical users can also check the .ots proof with the official OpenTimestamps client — a tool we did not write.

What exactly does a green result prove?

Two things, and two only: this data existed no later than the indicated block, and it has not been modified since. The witness does not say whether the content is accurate or sincere — that is up to the institution that issued it, as with a paper document.

A doubt, a question, a red result? The right reflex is always the same: contact the institution that handed you the witness.
